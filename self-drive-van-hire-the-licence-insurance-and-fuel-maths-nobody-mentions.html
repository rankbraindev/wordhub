Self-Drive Van Hire: The Licence, Insurance and Fuel Maths Nobody Mentions

There is a moment familiar to almost everyone who has moved home in London. You have a flat full of boxes, a completion date closing in, and a browser with fourteen tabs open. Somewhere in that pile is a van hire site advertising a Transit for £69 a day, and next to it sits a removals quote for three times that. The arithmetic looks settled before you have even read the terms. Hire the van, rope in a mate, buy them dinner, keep the difference.

It is a decision Londoners face more often than most. Renters in the capital move roughly every 2.5 years on average, with around 404,600 rental properties changing hands in 2024 alone — and the option of simply loading up the family car is unavailable to a great many of them, since 42.1% of London households have no access to a car or van at all, the highest proportion of any English region and one that rises to 66.9% in Islington and 66.4% in Tower Hamlets.

That instinct to hire is reasonable. It is also built on a single number that turns out to be the least reliable figure in the whole transaction. The advertised day rate is not a price so much as an opening position — accurate as far as it goes, but silent on the deposit held against your card, the excess you carry if the wing mirror meets a bollard on a narrow Hackney side street, the surcharge added because of your age, and the diesel a loaded Luton drinks at roughly 18 miles to the gallon in stop-start traffic. With diesel averaging 174.0p a litre in August 2026, that consumption works out at close to 44p per mile before a single other cost is counted. None of these are hidden exactly. They are simply spread across three different pages of terms that nobody reads at eleven o'clock at night.

This is not an argument against hiring a van. For plenty of jobs it remains comfortably the cheapest option, and for some it is the only sensible one. It is an argument for knowing the real number before you commit, because the gap between the headline rate and the amount that eventually leaves your account can be the difference between a shrewd decision and an expensive Saturday.

What follows works through that gap in order. We start with what the day rate genuinely buys and the surprisingly strict clock it runs on. Then the counter checks that can stop a confirmed booking dead on collection morning, the excess sitting behind the phrase "insurance included", and the two separate meters — fuel and mileage — ticking away while you drive. By the end you will be able to assemble your own total, and work out precisely where your break-even point sits.

The £69 Van That Isn't: Reading a Day Rate Properly

Van hire pricing is refreshingly transparent right up to the point where it isn't. Every firm publishes a rate, every rate is genuinely available, and every rate describes a narrower thing than most customers assume. Understanding what that figure actually covers is the foundation for everything else, because each cost that follows attaches itself to this one.

Rates in 2026 sit on a fairly predictable ladder, sorted by how much load space you are renting rather than by any other quality of the vehicle:

  • Small van, Kangoo or Caddy class: around £55 to £75 per day
  • Medium short-wheelbase van, Transit Custom or Trafic class: around £75 to £100
  • Large long-wheelbase van, Sprinter or Master class: around £95 to £130
  • Luton box van with a tail lift: around £130 to £180

Two adjustments apply before those numbers mean anything to a Londoner. The first is VAT, charged at the standard 20% rate, which many hire companies still quote separately, particularly the independents who deal heavily with trade customers. A £95 large van is £114 once the tax lands, and that fifth on top has a habit of arriving as a surprise at the counter rather than on the booking page. The second is geography. Depot rates in the capital and the wider South East typically run around £20 a day above the Midlands equivalent for the same vehicle, a premium driven by land costs, congestion and demand rather than anything about the van itself. The Luton advertised at £130 by a firm in Leicester is closer to £150 by the time you find one in Tottenham or Battersea.

The 24-Hour Clock, and Why It Rarely Suits a Move

Here is the detail that catches out more first-time hirers than any other: a rental day is a strict 24-hour block, not a calendar day. Collect at 10am on Saturday and the clock expires at 10am on Sunday. Return the van at midday and you have entered a second 24-hour period, and you will be charged for two full days. Enterprise states the rule plainly in its own pricing guidance: return at 9:45 the following morning and you pay for one day; return at noon and you pay for two.

Why does this matter so much for a house move specifically? Because moves overrun. Keys are late, the previous occupants are still loading their own van, the lift in the block is out, or the sofa turns out not to clear the stairwell without the door coming off its hinges. A move that was comfortably going to finish by six o'clock finishes at nine, by which point the depot has closed and your one-day hire has become a two-day hire whether you use the second day or not.

There are ways to work with this rather than against it. Many depots operate an out-of-hours key drop, though you remain responsible for the vehicle until it is checked in the following morning. Some firms, Arnold Clark among them, offer hourly hire within office opening hours, with a minimum hire period of just one hour, which suits a single-item collection far better than a full day. And longer bookings get cheaper fast on a per-day basis — a van at £69 for one or two days can drop to around £55 a day over six to ten days, and as low as £46 a day on hires of eleven to twenty days, which changes the calculation entirely if you are moving gradually rather than in one push. The same logic applies if you are not moving house at all but clearing a backlog out of a flat that has run out of room, where the job is a series of short trips spread over a fortnight rather than one long day. Pre-paying can shave more off still, with some national chains offering around 15% off van hire booked and paid in advance.

The Money You Need But Don't Spend

Separate from the hire price sits the security deposit, held against your card for the duration and released after the van is checked back in. Expect somewhere between £150 and £500 depending on the operator and the vehicle, with £200 a common fixed figure at the larger chains. Some firms reduce the deposit if you buy their damage waiver, so a £500 hold might drop to £250.

This is not a cost, strictly speaking. You get it back. But it does mean the headroom you need on your card is considerably higher than the price you agreed, the funds are frozen for several days after the hire ends while the release works its way through, and anyone moving on a tight budget in the same week they have handed over a rental deposit and a first month's rent may find the timing genuinely awkward.

So the £69 van is real. It is simply £83 with VAT, £103 if the move slips past the return time, and it requires £583 of available credit on the day. That is the number worth carrying forward — and it is worth noting that most people only start reading van hire terms this closely after they have already glanced at some man and van quotes and decided that driving themselves must be the cheaper route. Whether that assumption survives contact with the details is what the rest of this article is for.

Confirmed Online, Refused at the Counter: The Checks Nobody Warns You About

A van hire booking is not the same as a van hire. Between the two sits a counter, a member of staff, and a checklist that has very little to do with whether you can drive and quite a lot to do with what the firm's insurers will accept. Plenty of people discover this on collection morning, boxes already stacked by the front door, when a booking confirmed three weeks earlier quietly falls apart.

The frustrating part is that none of these criteria are secret. They are published, they are consistent within each company, and they vary wildly between companies — which is exactly why assuming that the terms you read at one firm apply at the next is such a costly habit.

Age Is a Bigger Barrier Than You Think

Car hire has trained most of us to expect a modest young driver surcharge and little else. Van hire is stricter, and the thresholds sit higher than people anticipate.

Minimum ages across the market range considerably. Some independents will hire to a driver of 21 or 23 — Arnold Clark, for instance, sets its minimum driver age at 23. The large national chains commonly set the floor at 25. And several go further by tiering their fleet, restricting the biggest vehicles to older drivers — Enterprise, for instance, requires drivers to be over 30 for a Luton van or a full-size MPV, even though 25 is enough for a standard panel van.

Read that restriction again in the context of a typical move, because it produces an odd result. The people most likely to need the largest van are often shifting a shared house or a first family home in their late twenties. That is precisely the group the Luton is closed to at some firms. You can be a perfectly experienced driver, fully able to pay, and still find that the only vehicle big enough to do your move in one trip is unavailable to you at that depot.

Where a surcharge does apply rather than an outright refusal, budget £20 to £40 per day for it. Drivers under 25 pay it, and so do drivers over 69 at many firms. Arnold Clark's young driver surcharge is a flat £25, applied in full on any rental up to 24 hours and pro-rated on hourly bookings. On a £75 medium van that is a swing of up to fifty per cent on the base rate.

Licence History, Points and the Check Code

Beyond age, firms want to see a driving history they consider settled. Most require that you have held a full licence for at least one year, and some insist on two. A licence issued eight months ago will not do, regardless of how well you drive.

Endorsements are handled with more discretion than rule. Arnold Clark, as one example, asks that penalty points be declared in advance and states plainly that acceptance is at branch discretion. That phrase does a lot of work. It means three points for speeding might pass without comment at one depot and prompt a refusal at another, and it means the only reliable approach is to ring ahead and ask rather than gamble on the day.

The documents themselves are more demanding than car hire, and one of them cannot be produced on the spot:

  • A valid full driving licence, physical card
  • A DVLA licence check code, generated in advance on GOV.UK and valid for a limited window
  • Proof of address, usually a utility bill or bank statement dated within the last three months
  • Photo identification such as a passport, where the licence alone is not accepted
  • A payment card in the driver's own name, for both the hire and the deposit

That check code is the classic stumbling block. It is valid for 21 days from the moment it is generated, it has to be requested online, and a fair number of people arrive at the depot having never heard of it. Generate it the day before, not the morning of.

Additional drivers are worth planning for too. Sharing the driving on a longer move sounds sensible until you learn that each extra name typically costs around £10 per day and must clear the same age, licence and document checks independently. The friend helping you move cannot simply take a turn at the wheel.

All of which points to something the price comparison rarely captures. For a 23-year-old who needs a Luton, or a driver who passed their test last autumn, self-drive hire is not an expensive option — it is not an option at all. The man and van quotes sitting in the other browser tab carry no age band, no licence tenure requirement and no check code, because the eligibility question simply never arises. That is a genuine part of their value, and it belongs in the comparison alongside the pounds — as do the other routes open to someone who cannot or would rather not drive, from borrowing a vehicle to booking a crew who bring their own.

"Insurance Included" — Three Words Doing an Enormous Amount of Heavy Lifting

Every van hire listing carries some version of the same reassurance. Fully comprehensive insurance included. Breakdown cover as standard. It reads like the risk has been handled, and it prompts most people to move straight past it to the price.

It has not been handled. It has been capped, and the cap is the part that matters.

The Number Behind the Reassurance

The cover included with your hire comes with an excess: the amount you pay towards any damage before the waiver takes over. On hire vans that excess is substantial. Typical figures run from £1,000 to £2,000, and some operators set it considerably higher — Arnold Clark's standard vehicle damage excess is £3,000, reducible to £1,000 only if you buy their waiver. Enterprise sets its excess between £1,750 and £2,250 depending on the vehicle, reducible to £100–£250 with additional protection.

Sit with that for a moment against the cost of the hire. A £69 van carries a potential liability of £1,250 at a firm like A to B Self Drive. A kerbed alloy, a scraped flank against a wall on a tight approach, a reversing knock in a supermarket car park — none of these are exotic misfortunes, and any of them can produce a repair bill that comfortably exceeds the excess. Meaning you pay the full excess, not the repair cost. You are effectively self-insuring the first thousand-odd pounds of anything that happens to a large, unfamiliar vehicle you will drive for one day of your life.

Reducing that exposure costs money. An excess waiver typically runs £15 to £30 per day and brings the excess down to somewhere between £100 and £250, often reducing the security deposit at the same time. It is genuinely good value as insurance products go. It also has an awkward effect on the maths: a £69 van with a £15 waiver is £84, and £101 with VAT. The advertised rate has risen by nearly half before you have turned a wheel, and this is the point at which the comparison with paying someone else starts to wobble.

The Parts of the Van the Waiver Won't Touch

Here is where it gets genuinely uncomfortable. Even with the waiver purchased, hire agreements almost universally carve out specific areas of the vehicle for which you remain fully liable, whatever you have paid. The standard exclusions are:

  • The roof and any overhead damage
  • Windscreens and all glass
  • Tyres and wheels
  • The undercarriage
  • Damage to the interior of the load bay, including stains and tears

Look at that list and then think about how a van actually gets damaged during a house move. Almost every item on it describes a first-timer's mistake rather than a road traffic accident.

The roof exclusion is the brutal one. A Luton stands around 3.5 metres tall, and a driver who has spent their life in a hatchback has no instinct for that height whatsoever. Multi-storey car parks in central London are commonly barriered at 2 metres. Height restrictions under railway arches, hotel forecourts, retail park entrances and countless estate car parks sit well below what you are driving. This is not a rare mishap either: Network Rail recorded 1,666 bridge strikes between April 2024 and March 2025, roughly one every five hours, at an average clean-up cost of £13,000 per incident and around £23m a year across the network. Most of those involve HGVs and buses rather than hire vans, but the severity is worth registering — a single strike on one Grantham bridge caused £423,000 of damage. A to B Self Drive states the position with admirable bluntness: drive under a low bridge and damage the roof, and the hirer is fully liable for repairs. No waiver, no cap, no argument.

The load bay exclusion catches people just as often, if less dramatically. Drag an unprotected chest of drawers across the floor, let a bike pedal gouge the side lining, or split a bottle of olive oil in a badly packed kitchen box, and you are looking at a cleaning or repair charge on a surface you probably never inspected on collection. Photograph the load bay before you load it, not just the exterior panels.

None of this makes van hire a bad deal. It makes it a deal in which you are holding a category of risk that most people have not consciously agreed to hold. And that is the quiet structural difference with the alternative: the figures in man and van quotes are inclusive of the operator's own vehicle cover and goods-in-transit protection, so a kerbed tyre or a misjudged height barrier is their problem, priced into their day rate long before you rang them. You are not just buying labour. You are buying your way out of a £1,250 exposure that never appears on either quote.
